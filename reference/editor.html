On silence between two people

I. The two kinds of silence

There is a silence that unsettles and another that rests. The difference is not in its duration but in what each person brings to that blank space that opens between two people who have known each other long enough to stop pretending.

I have been thinking about this for weeks — since the afternoon we sat on the terrace and said almost nothing for an hour, and I left feeling like we had spoken more honestly than in any conversation we'd had in months.

Silence can also be a form of attention. Not the absence of words but the presence of something that doesn't need to be named to exist.

What attention actually means

Most people confuse attention with response. We think that paying attention means reacting — nodding, asking follow-up questions, offering our own story in return. But there is another kind of attention that works differently. It holds space without filling it.

The philosopher Simone Weil wrote that attention is the rarest and purest form of generosity. She meant something specific: the capacity to receive what another person is, without immediately translating it into something familiar. Without making it about you.

What I noticed that afternoon

Three things stayed with me from that hour on the terrace:

  • The way you looked at the garden without needing to comment on it
  • The moment you laughed at something — I still don't know what — and didn't explain it
  • How you refilled my glass without asking if I wanted more

None of these are dramatic. That's the point. Attention at its most real operates below the threshold of the noticeable. It's only afterward, walking home, that you realize something was given to you.

The problem with being understood

Being understood is not always comfortable. Sometimes it is the most exposed feeling there is — more vulnerable than being seen, because being understood implies that the other person has done work. They have paid a cost. And now you owe them something: the acknowledgment that they got it right.

That afternoon I felt understood. And for a moment I wanted to deflect it — to say something funny, to change the subject, to restore the comfortable distance that misunderstanding provides.[1]

On writing as a form of return

I am writing this because I couldn't say it then. Not because the words weren't there — they were — but because saying them would have required me to be present in a way I wasn't ready for.

Writing is the conversation I have with myself about the conversations I couldn't finish out loud.

There is something in the delay that helps. The letter that arrives three days after the event it describes. The note left on the table before someone wakes up. Time changes what can be said — not because the truth changes, but because we do.

II. What this letter is

This is not an apology. It is not an explanation. It is closer to what happens when you return to a place you left in a hurry and finally look at it properly — not to reclaim it, but to give it the attention it deserved the first time.

  1. It is a record of something I noticed
  2. It is an attempt to be present, retroactively
  3. It is, I suppose, a form of gratitude

I don't know if you'll read this. I don't know if it matters whether you do. Some things need to be written before they can be released — not sent, just written. The sending is secondary. The act of putting it into words is what changes something.[2]

[1] This impulse to deflect understanding is what Sartre called mauvaise foi — bad faith. The refusal to be pinned down, to be known, even by those we trust most.
[2] See also: Winnicott on the capacity to be alone in the presence of another. The paradox is that writing to someone is sometimes the most honest way of being with them.
